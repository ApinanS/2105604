Lecture 5-1

What is the heterogeneous phase reaction?

Lecture 5-1 · the plan

Five steps to read any multiphase reactor

  1. One rule for every systemthe reaction lives in one phase only
  2. Systems with a solidsolid reactant versus solid catalyst
  3. Fluid–fluid systems, L–L and G–Lwhich reactant must dissolve first
  4. Three-phase systems, L–L–S and G–L–Sdissolve first, then reach the catalyst
  5. The path a reactant travelshow many steps sit in series before reaction

Heterogeneous reaction

The reaction happens in one phase

  • However many phases are present, the reaction usually runs in only one of them.
  • If the reactants sit in different phases, one must first dissolve into the other's phase.

Liquid–Solid · Gas–Solid reaction

A solid can be a reactant or a catalyst

The rule says one phase hosts the reaction. With a solid present, which phase depends on the solid's role.

Solid reactant

The solid dissolves or sublimes, then reacts in the fluid phase.

Solid catalytic reaction

The reaction runs at active sites in the catalyst pores (Lectures 3–4).

Liquid–Liquid reaction · homogeneous catalyst

Transesterification: oil must dissolve in methanol

$\text{Triglyceride} + 3\,\mathrm{CH_3OH}\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{NaOH}\ }\ 3\,\text{FAME} + \text{Glycerol}$

FAME — fatty acid methyl ester (biodiesel).

  • Methanol + catalyst and the triglyceride oil do not mix: two liquid phases.
  • Triglyceride must dissolve into methanol before it reacts.

yellow drops: methanol + NaOH · blue: triglyceride

Gas–Liquid reaction

The gas must dissolve before it reacts

Examples: hydrogenation, alkoxylation, oxidation, chlorination.

  • The reaction runs mainly in the liquid phase.
  • The gas reactant must first dissolve into the liquid.

white bubbles: gas fed by the sparger

Solid–Liquid–Liquid reaction · heterogeneous catalyst

Same oil, now on a solid catalyst

Same reaction as the L–L slide, solid catalyst$\text{Triglyceride} + 3\,\mathrm{CH_3OH}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{solid cat.}\ }\ 3\,\text{FAME} + \text{Glycerol}$
  • Triglyceride first dissolves into methanol.
  • It then reacts at the active sites of the solid catalyst.

grey dots: solid catalyst inside the methanol drops

Gas–Liquid–Solid reaction

Gas dissolves, then meets the catalyst

Examples: hydrotreating of plant oil; fermentation (cells act as the solid).

  • The reaction runs in the liquid that wets the catalyst and its active sites.
  • The gas reactant dissolves into the liquid, then reacts.

bubbles + liquid + grey catalyst particles

Rate expression for 2-phase reaction

Three rates in series

The profile falls in three places; each fall is driven by one rate.

$r_{md}=k_{md}\,a\,(C_{Adb}-C_{Adi})$1 · bulk dispersed phase → interface
$r_{mc}=k_{mc}\,a\,(C_{Aci}-C_{Acb})$2 · interface → bulk continuous phase

$k_{md},\,k_{mc}$

Eliminating the interface

Each step gives one concentrationstep 1 of 3

Three rates, previous slide$r_{md}=k_{md}a(C_{Adb}-C_{Adi})\quad r_{mc}=k_{mc}a(C_{Aci}-C_{Acb})\quad r_1=k_rC_{Bcb}C_{Acb}$
$r_{md}=r_{mc}=r_1\equiv r$steady state: nothing accumulates in the films
$C_{Acb}=\dfrac{r}{k_rC_{Bcb}}\qquad C_{Aci}=C_{Acb}+\dfrac{r}{k_{mc}a}$solve step 3, then step 2
$C_{Adi}=C_{Adb}-\dfrac{r}{k_{md}a}$solve step 1 for the dispersed side

Eliminating the interface

Join the two sides at the interfacestep 2 of 3

Interface equilibrium$C_{Aci}=K_{cd}\,C_{Adi}$
$\dfrac{r}{k_rC_{Bcb}}+\dfrac{r}{k_{mc}a}=K_{cd}\left(C_{Adb}-\dfrac{r}{k_{md}a}\right)$put both sides of step 1 into equilibrium
$\left(\dfrac{1}{k_rC_{Bcb}}+\dfrac{K_{cd}}{k_{md}a}+\dfrac{1}{k_{mc}a}\right)r=K_{cd}\,C_{Adb}$collect every $r$ term on the left

Eliminating the interface

Three resistances in seriesstep 3 of 3

divide by $K_{cd}$refers every resistance to the dispersed bulk
$$r=\frac{C_{Adb}}{\dfrac{1}{k_rK_{cd}C_{Bcb}}+\dfrac{1}{k_{md}a}+\dfrac{1}{K_{cd}k_{mc}a}}=k_{app}\,C_{Adb}$$

$k_{app}$ — apparent rate constant (1/s): one measurable number lumping all three steps.

Valid at steady state, with equilibrium at the interface and reaction only in the continuous bulk.

Concentrations are the bulk values in each phase, not the mixture concentration.

Apparent kinetics for 2 phases

Mass transfer controlled

$$r=k_{mc}\,a\,K_{cd}\,C_{Adb}=k\,C_{Adb}$$
  • $k$ ignores B's concentration but depends on drop size ($a$) and impeller power.
  • Weakly temperature-sensitive (low activation energy).
  • $K_{cd}$, the solubility of A in the continuous phase, still sets the rate.
  • Predict $k_{mc}$ and $a$ from correlations and solubility; compare with the measured $k$.

Apparent kinetics for 2 phases

Reaction controlled

$$r=k_r\,K_{cd}\,C_{Bcb}\,C_{Adb}=k\,C_{Adb}$$
  • $k$ depends on B's concentration in the continuous phase, not on impeller power.
  • Strongly temperature-sensitive (high activation energy).
  • $K_{cd}$ still multiplies the rate.

Lecture 5-2 · summary

What section 5-2 settled

  • Two films and a reaction act as three resistances in series.
  • $r=k_{app}C_{Adb}$, with $k_{app}$ lumping all three steps.

Remark for multiphase reaction

Chemistry is fixed; transport is not

  • Unlike the chemical steps (adsorption, surface reaction, desorption), the physical rates change with scale and operating conditions.
  • So the apparent rate can differ from scale to scale.
  • The controlling step can differ too.

What is the rate expression used to design an industrial reactor?

Lecture 5-4 · the plan

From lab data to a plant-size reactor

  1. Where rate expressions come fromexperiments, validated one scale at a time
  2. What changes with scalephysical rates, and with them the limiting step
  3. What designers usethe apparent first-order rate of the limiting reactant
  4. When exact kinetics returnoptimizing a plant that already runs

Rate expression for design industrial reactor

Validated one scale at a time

  • Rate expressions are obtained from experimental data.
  • To design a commercial reactor, validate the rate expression against runs at several sizes.
  • The expression from each smaller reactor is used to design the next size.

Rate expression for design industrial reactor

The same chemistry, a different rate

5-3 ended on a warning: transport moves with scale. Here is what that does to the design data.

2-phase result, 5-2$\dfrac{1}{k_{app}}=\underbrace{\dfrac{1}{k_rK_{cd}C_{Bcb}}}_{\text{chemical}}+\underbrace{\dfrac{1}{k_{md}a}+\dfrac{1}{K_{cd}k_{mc}a}}_{\text{physical}}$
  • The chemical rate does not change with scale; the physical rate does.
  • So each scale can have a different limiting step, and the rate expression may change.

Rate expression for design industrial reactor

Design stage versus operating plant

During design

Exact (intrinsic) rate expressions are not used, even for multiphase reactions: the limiting step shifts with scale.

After commercialization

Exact rate expressions may be needed to optimize or improve the running process.

During design stage

The apparent rate of the limiting reactant

  • The apparent rate expression designs each size of reactor.
  • It is a function of the limiting reactant concentration only.
  • A simple power law is usually used; first order is usually assumed.
$$r'=k'_{app}\,C_{Ab}$$

$k'_{app}$ is refitted at each scale; $C_{Ab}$ — bulk concentration of the limiting reactant A.

Lecture 5

Key Takeaways

1

Find the phase that hosts the reaction; everything else must cross into it.

2

Write the steps as resistances in series; the largest one controls.

3

Use the bulk concentration of each phase: $r=k_{app}C_{Adb}$.

4

Diagnose the regime with stirring, temperature, $C_B$ and catalyst loading.

5

Design with an apparent first-order rate, refitted at every scale.
