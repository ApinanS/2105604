Classification

Four ideal reactors, four sets of assumptions

Batch

No flow in or out · well mixed · constant $V$ · unsteady.

Semi-batch

Inlet or outlet, not both · well mixed · $V$ grows · unsteady.

Stirred tank (CSTR)

Continuous stirred-tank reactor: both streams · perfectly mixed · steady.

Tubular (PFR)

Plug-flow reactor: both streams · no radial gradient · steady.

Each has a solid-catalytic twin: normalize the rate per unit volume ($-r_A$) or per kilogram of catalyst ($-r'_A$).

The master equation

General mole balance on species $j$

$$\underbrace{F_{j0}-F_j}_{\text{in}-\text{out}}+\underbrace{\int_V \sum_{i=1}^{R}\nu_{ij}\,r_i\,dV}_{\text{generation}}=\underbrace{\frac{dN_j}{dt}}_{\text{accumulation}}$$
$F_{j0},\,F_j$
molar flow of $j$ entering and leaving
mol/s
$\nu_{ij}$
stoichiometric coefficient of $j$ in reaction $i$ — negative if consumed
–
$r_i$
rate of reaction $i$ per unit volume, one of $R$ reactions
mol/(m³·s)
$N_j$
moles of $j$ held inside the reactor right now
mol
$V$
reaction volume the balance is taken over
m³

The balance needs $\sum_i\nu_{ij}r_i$. With two reactions running, that sum is where students lose the sign.

Rate & stoichiometry

One species, several reactions

$$\text{A}+2\text{B}\to 4\text{C}+\text{D}\quad r_1=k_1 C_A C_B^2$$ $$2\text{A}+\text{B}\to \text{U}+3\text{G}\quad r_2=k_2 C_A^2 C_B$$
$$r_A=\nu_{1A}r_1+\nu_{2A}r_2=-r_1-2r_2$$
Reading the signs
  • $\nu_{ij}<0$ → consumed; $\nu_{ij}>0$ → generated
  • A carries $-1$ in reaction 1 and $-2$ in reaction 2, so both terms subtract
  • The subscript $1$ in $r_1$ counts the reaction, never the species

Temperature dependence

Arrhenius law, written the way the solver wants it

Rate constant

$$k(T)=Ae^{-E/RT}=k(T_0)\,e^{\frac{E}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_0}-\frac1T\right)}$$

Equilibrium constant

$$K_e(T)=K_e(T_1)\,e^{\frac{\Delta H_{rxn}^0}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac1T\right)}$$

$E$
activation energy of the reaction
J/mol
$T_0$
reference temperature at which $k$ was measured
K
$\Delta H_{rxn}^0$
standard heat of reaction — sets which way $K_e$ moves
J/mol

The reference form is literally the script line k1=k10*exp((E1/R)*((1/(T0+273))-(1/(T+273)))).

What the balance is drawn around

Four reactors, and the one assumption that separates each pair

Batch

closed

Charge it, close it, wait. Nothing crosses the boundary, so composition depends on time alone.

$\dfrac{dN_j}{dt}=\Big(\textstyle\sum_i\nu_{ij}r_i\Big)V$

Semi-batch

fed, not drained

One stream in, none out — so $V$ grows as you go. The usual reason: keep one reactant always scarce.

$\dfrac{dN_j}{dt}=F_{j0}+\Big(\textstyle\sum_i\nu_{ij}r_i\Big)V$

CSTR

open, perfectly mixed

Steady flow through a stirred tank. Mixing is complete, so the exit stream is the contents — and the algebra has no derivative left.

$F_{j0}-F_j+\Big(\textstyle\sum_i\nu_{ij}r_i\Big)V=0$

PFR / PBR

open, no back-mixing

Steady flow, no mixing along the tube. Composition changes with position. Pack it with catalyst and $W$ replaces $V$.

$\dfrac{dF_j}{dV}=\textstyle\sum_i\nu_{ij}r_i$

Lecture 5-1

What section 5-1 settled

  • There is one mole balance. A reactor is a set of assumptions that deletes terms from it.
  • Signs come from $\nu_{ij}$ alone: negative consumes, positive generates, and every reaction contributes.
  • Steady state kills $dN_j/dt$ → algebra; everything else stays a differential equation.
  • $k$ follows Arrhenius, and the reference-temperature form is what you type into the solver.

Design equation

Batch reactor: homogeneous and solid-catalytic

General balance, section 5-1, with both flow terms zero$\displaystyle\int_V\sum_i\nu_{ij}r_i\,dV=\frac{dN_j}{dt}$
Homogeneous, constant $V$
$$\frac{1}{V}\frac{dN_j}{dt}=\sum_i \nu_{ij}r_i\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{dC_j}{dt}=\sum_i \nu_{ij}r_i$$
Solid-catalytic, rate per kg catalyst
$$\frac{dN_j}{dt}=\sum_i \nu_{ij}r'_i\,W_{cat},\qquad C_j=\frac{N_j}{V}$$

Only a constant $V$ may cross the derivative. In 5-3 it moves, and this step dies.

One balance per species is easy. Keeping six of them consistent is what a solver is for.

The equations this example needs

Six mole balances, constant $V$
$$\begin{aligned} \tfrac{dC_A}{dt}&=-r_1-2r_2 & \tfrac{dC_B}{dt}&=-2r_1\\ \tfrac{dC_C}{dt}&=r_1-r_2 & \tfrac{dC_D}{dt}&=3r_1\\ \tfrac{dC_E}{dt}&=r_2 & \tfrac{dC_F}{dt}&=r_2 \end{aligned}$$
Rate laws, kinetics and start point
$$r_1=k_1C_AC_B^2-k_2C_CC_D^3,\quad r_2=k_3C_A^2C_C$$
$$k_i=k_{i0}\,e^{\frac{E_i}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_0}-\frac1T\right)},\quad C_j(0)=C_{j0}$$

Eleven relations for eleven unknowns: six $C_j$, two rates, three $k_i$. Count before you type.

Lecture 5-2

What section 5-2 settled

  • Constant volume is the whole trick: it turns a mole balance into $dC_j/dt=\sum_i\nu_{ij}r_i$.
  • Write one balance per species, add the rate laws and the property relations, then integrate.
  • Batch time is read off the profile, not solved for — you integrate until $X$ is where you want it.
  • Temperature is never a free lever: it moves conversion and selectivity in opposite directions.

In a liquid the volumetric flow is a constant you can divide by. In a gas it is another unknown.

Why gas reactors are different

Volumetric flow moves with moles, pressure and temperature

Liquid phase
$$v=v_0\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;C_j=\frac{F_j}{v_0}$$
Gas phase, ideal
$$v=v_0\left(\frac{P_0}{P}\right)\!\left(\frac{T}{T_0}\right)\!\left(\frac{F_T}{F_{T0}}\right)$$

$F_T=\sum_j F_j$ is the total molar flow; $F_{T0}$ is its inlet value.

Both reactions here make more moles than they consume, so $F_T$ rises, the stream expands, every concentration is diluted, and the tube must be longer than the liquid case.

The one place the house rule bends

When the unknown is the end of the integration

The rule, and its exception

EQUATRAN will not put FIND or RESET on an INTEGRAL bound — INTEGRAL and FIND cannot share a program, and a limit is not a variable the solver can iterate.

$$\texttt{FUNCTION PFR(Vol; XA1, ...)}$$
What the wrapper buys
  • Inside: the six ODEs, marched from 0 to Vol
  • Outside: one algebraic relation, volume in → conversion out
  • So RESET Vol BY sp can iterate on it like any other unknown

The CSTR in 5-4 needed no function: it is algebraic already. Only an initial-value problem whose end point is the answer needs this.

Lecture 5-5

What section 5-5 settled

  • A PFR is an initial-value problem in volume: integrate $dF_j/dV$ from the feed to the exit.
  • For a gas, $v$ is recomputed every step from $P$, $T$ and total moles — never assumed constant.
  • Making moles dilutes the stream, and dilution costs volume even at fixed temperature.
  • At positive order a PFR always beats a CSTR, and the gap grows with the target conversion.

In the bracket, 1.75 is the inertial term and $150(1-\Phi)/N_\text{Re}$ the viscous one: below $N_\text{Re}\approx10$, $\Delta P\propto u_0$; above $\approx1000$, $\Delta P\propto u_0^2$.

The Ergun equation needs $\rho$, and $\rho$ moves as pressure falls and moles change. One law supplies both.

Ideal gas · estimating gas properties

Density and volumetric flow, at any point in the bed

$$P_0v_0=F_{T0}RT_0,\qquad Pv=F_TRT$$
$$\rho=\frac{\dot m}{v}=\rho_0\frac{v_0}{v}=\rho_0\frac{P}{P_0}\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)\frac{F_{T0}}{F_T}$$
$$\frac{v}{v_0}=\left(\frac{P_0}{P}\right)\left(\frac{T}{T_0}\right)\left(\frac{F_T}{F_{T0}}\right)$$

Mass is conserved, moles are not — $\dot m$ is fixed, so $v$ and $\rho$ always move opposite ways.

What section 5-6 settled

  • A fixed bed is a PFR whose independent variable is catalyst weight: $dF_j/dW=\sum_i\nu_{ij}r'_i$.
  • Pressure drop is not a correction — it is one more state integrated alongside the flows.

Along the catalyst weight

Lecture 5

Key Takeaways

1

Start from the one general mole balance, then delete the terms your reactor's assumptions kill.

2

Write balance → rate law → property relations, and only then integrate or root-find.

3

For gas beds always carry volume change and pressure drop — either can dominate the design.

4

Check whether transport limits you before you trust a rate constant or an order.

5

Validate every numerical answer against a closed form or a criterion you can compute by hand.
