How does a CSTR reach its steady state — and is that approach safe?

The steady state is what the design promises. Start-up is the part of the day the operator actually has to survive.

A steady-state design says where the reactor ends. It says nothing about the road there.

The dangerous minutes are the first ones

The approach, not just the destination

  • The vessel is charged cold or hot, then feed is started — neither composition nor temperature is at its final value.
  • A temperature overshoot can degrade product, foul a catalyst, or boil a solvent.
  • A large enough overshoot leaves the reactor's safe operating region entirely.
The steady state is the same whatever you charge. The peak on the way there is not.
The clearest picture is the temperature–concentration phase plane: plot $T$ against $C_A$ and watch the path curl into its end point.

Mixed flow, but time-dependent

What survives when nothing is deleted

Mole balance — every term present

$$\frac{dN_i}{dt}=F_{i0}-F_i+\Big(\sum_j\nu_{ij}r_j\Big)V$$

Perfect mixing: the outlet is the tank, so $F_i=C_iv_0$ with $C_i=N_i/V$.

Energy balance — every term present

$$\Big(\textstyle\sum_i N_iC_{p,i}\Big)\frac{dT}{dt}=\dot Q+\dot W_s-\sum_j r_jV\Delta H_{Rxj}-\sum_i F_{i0}C_{p,i}(T-T_F)$$
Set both derivatives to zero and you recover the algebraic CSTR of Lecture 5. Keeping them is the whole of this section.

The equations this example needs

Six balances, accumulation form
$$\frac{dN_i}{dt}=F_{i0}-F_i+\Big(\sum_j\nu_{ij}r_j\Big)V$$
$$C_i=\frac{N_i}{V},\qquad F_i=C_i\,v_0$$

Only A and B have a non-zero $F_{i0}$; the four products enter at zero.

One energy balance, feed term included
$$\Big(\textstyle\sum_i N_iC_{p,i}\Big)\tfrac{dT}{dt}=\dot Q-\textstyle\sum_j r_jV\Delta H_{Rxj}-\textstyle\sum_i F_{i0}C_{p,i}(T-T_F)$$
$$r_1=k_1C_AC_B^2-k_2C_CC_D^3,\quad r_2=k_3C_A^2C_C$$

Seven states: six $N_i$ and $T$; $V$ is fixed. The jacket is $\dot Q=UA(T_{cw}-T)$.

Lecture 8-2

When does a reactor run away, and how much cooling stops it?

Runaway is not a chemistry question. It is a race between a curve that bends upward and a line that does not.

Lecture 8-2

Generation against removal

  1. The two curvesHeat made and heat taken away, on one axis.
  2. Three geometriesNo crossing, one tangency, two crossings.
  3. The critical area problemSizing a jacket so the reactor has somewhere to sit.
  4. Where you start mattersWhy two crossings are necessary but not sufficient.

Every cooling failure so far has had the same shape. Here is the picture behind all of them.

The Semenov picture

One curve bends, the other does not

$$\dot G(T)=F_{A0}(-\Delta H_{Rx})\,X(T),\qquad X=\frac{Da}{1+Da}$$
$$Da=k_0\tau\,e^{-E/RT},\qquad \dot R(T)=UA\,(T-T_a)$$
$\dot G$ is S-shaped: exponential while $X$ is small, then flat once the feed is fully converted.
$\dot R$ is a straight line through $T_a$, and its slope is the heat-transfer area you buy.

What the picture can look like

Three ways the line can meet the curve

No crossing

Removal is always below generation. Nothing stops the rise: the reactor runs away from any starting point.

One tangency

The line just touches the curve. This is the critical case, and the area that produces it is the smallest safe jacket.

Two crossings

A low stable steady state and a middle unstable one. Below the middle point the reactor returns; above it, it ignites.

Design for the third case with margin. A jacket sized exactly at tangency has no room for a fouled wall or a warm summer.

Worked example · sizing the jacket

How much wall does this reactor need?

  • First-order exothermic reaction in a stirred tank, $E=90$ kJ/mol and $k_0\tau=8\times10^{12}$.
  • Full conversion would release $F_{A0}(-\Delta H_{Rx})=140$ kW.
  • Coolant at $T_a=35$ °C; wall coefficient 0.6 kW/(m²·K), plus 0.05 kW/K of ambient loss.
  • Contents hold $\sum N_iC_{p,i}=4$ kJ/K; the vessel is charged at 55 °C.
$$\Big(\textstyle\sum_i N_iC_{p,i}\Big)\frac{dT}{dt}=\dot G(T)-\dot R(T)$$
Find: the smallest heat-transfer area that keeps this charge from igniting.

One state, one equation — the smallest reactor model in this lecture, and the sharpest.

Set up first, solve second

The equations this example needs

The lumped energy balance
$$\Big(\textstyle\sum_i N_iC_{p,i}\Big)\frac{dT}{dt}=\dot G(T)-\dot R(T),\quad T(0)=T_{ch}$$
$$\dot G=Q_{max}\frac{Da}{1+Da},\qquad Da=k_0\tau\,e^{-E/R(T+273)}$$
The removal line, and what to look for
$$\dot R=(U\!A_{wall}+U\!A_{loss})(T-T_a)$$
The crossings are the roots of $\dot G=\dot R$. That is a FIND problem, and EQUATRAN will not let it share a file with INTEGRAL.

So: integrate at one area, look at the answer, change the area, integrate again.

What section 8-2 settled

  • Runaway is a geometry: an S-shaped generation curve against a straight removal line.
  • The jacket area is the slope of that line, so sizing a jacket is choosing a slope.
  • Tangency gives the critical area; anything smaller has no cool steady state at all.

How do we solve a tubular reactor that is not yet steady?

Take the balance of 8-1 and apply it to a slice of tube instead of the whole vessel.

A balance on a thin slice

Two independent variables, $t$ and $V$

Mole balance on the slice $dV$

$$\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial V}+\sum_j\nu_{ij}r_j$$

Energy balance on the same slice

$$\Big(\textstyle\sum_i C_iC_{p,i}\Big)\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=-\Big(\textstyle\sum_i F_iC_{p,i}\Big)\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}-\sum_j r_j\Delta H_{Rxj}$$
PDE = partial differential equation: the state depends on where you are in the tube and on when you look.

Set both time derivatives to zero and you recover the steady PFR of Lecture 5.

Trading one PDE for many ODEs

Cut the tube into nodes, keep time continuous

$V\to V_1,V_2,\dots,V_n$ with spacing $\Delta V$a grid replaces the continuous length
$\dfrac{\partial F_i}{\partial V}\approx\dfrac{F_{i,j}-F_{i,j-1}}{\Delta V}$flow comes from upstream, so difference backwards
$\dfrac{dN_{i,j}}{dt}=\Delta V\!\sum_j\nu_{ij}r_j-(F_{i,j}-F_{i,j-1})$each node is now an ordinary differential equation
Node 1 is the inlet and is not integrated: it simply holds the feed. Every other node is a small stirred tank fed by the one before it.

Six species over $n$ nodes plus one temperature each: $7(n-1)$ ODEs, all marched together.

The equations this example needs

One balance per species, per node
$$\frac{dN_{i,j}}{dt}=\Delta V\sum_j\nu_{ij}r_{j}-\big(F_{i,j}-F_{i,j-1}\big)$$
$$C_{i,j}=\frac{N_{i,j}}{\Delta V},\qquad F_{i,j}=C_{i,j}\,v$$

Node 1 is not integrated: $F_{i,1}=F_{i0}$ and $T_1=T_F$.

One energy balance per node
$$\Big(\textstyle\sum_i C_{i,j}C_{p,i}\Big)\frac{dT_j}{dt}=-\textstyle\sum_j r_{j}\Delta H_{Rxj}-\frac{T_j-T_{j-1}}{\Delta V}\Big(\textstyle\sum_i F_{i,j}C_{p,i}\Big)$$
$$r_1=k_1C_AC_B^2-k_2C_CC_D^3,\quad r_2=k_3C_A^2C_C$$

Seventy ordinary differential equations, from two partial ones. The grid is what made the trade.

Lecture 8-3

What section 8-3 settled

  • An unsteady tube depends on time and position, so its balances are partial differential equations.
  • The method of lines discretises position only, leaving a large set of ordinary equations in time.
  • Difference the flow terms backwards: in a tube, information travels one way.
  • A grid-dependent answer is not an answer — refine until it stops moving.

The chemistry happens on a solid now, so the natural measure of the reactor is its mass, not its volume.

Packed bed reactor

From volume to catalyst weight

$$\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial W}\rho_b+\sum_j\nu_{ij}r_{sj}\,\rho_b$$

$r_{sj}$ is the rate per kilogram of catalyst, so $\rho_b r_{sj}$ is a rate per unit volume.

$W$
catalyst weight measured from the bed inlet
kg
$r_{sj}$
rate of reaction $j$ per kilogram of catalyst
mol/(kg·s)
$\rho_b$
bed density — catalyst mass per unit reactor volume
kg/m³
$\Phi$
bed voidage: the fraction of the volume that is gas
–
Write $r_s$, never $r'$: EQUATRAN reads a prime as a time derivative, so the primed notation of the textbook will not survive contact with the solver.

The new equation

Pressure falls, so the gas speeds up

$$\frac{dP}{dz}=-\frac{G}{\rho\,d_p}\left(\frac{1-\Phi}{\Phi^3}\right)\left[\frac{150(1-\Phi)\mu}{d_p}+1.75\,G\right]$$
$G=\rho u$ is fixed by the mass fedmass flux cannot change along a closed tube
$\rho$ falls as $P$ falls, so $u$ risesthe same mass through a lighter gas moves faster
faster gas $\Rightarrow$ shorter residence $\Rightarrow$ less conversionpressure drop is a conversion problem, not just a pump one

Both bracketed terms carry $1/d_p$, which is why pellet size is the strongest handle here.

Lecture 8-4

What section 8-4 settled

  • A packed bed is the 8-3 model with the rate written per kilogram of catalyst.
  • Ergun adds one equation, and that equation reaches every concentration through the velocity.
  • Pressure drop is a conversion problem before it is a pump problem.

Lecture 8

Key Takeaways

1

A flow reactor starting up keeps both derivatives — and its peak is invisible in a steady-state balance.

2

The end state belongs to the feed and the jacket; the excursion belongs to the charge. Size against the excursion.

3

Read runaway as geometry: an S-shaped generation curve against a removal line whose slope you buy, or lose to fouling.

4

For a tube, discretise position, keep time continuous, difference backwards, then refine until the answer stops moving.

5

Cooling a bed moves its hot spot as well as lowering it. Measure where the peak is, not where it is convenient.
