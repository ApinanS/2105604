Lecture 2-1

What is the rate expression for a reaction controlled by an elementary single reaction?

Definition

What is an elementary reaction?

Single step

The reaction happens in one step — reactants convert to products directly, with no detectable intermediate.

Collision-driven

The rate is set by the collision of reactant molecules in the system (2 or 3 reactants meeting).

Order = stoichiometry

Because there is one step, the reaction order equals the molecularity read from the balanced equation.

Homogeneous, non-catalytic: everything happens in a single fluid phase.

Before we write an equation

What does the reaction rate depend on?

✓ Reactant concentration

More reactant → more collisions → faster rate. This is the heart of the rate law.

✓ Temperature

Sets the fraction of collisions energetic enough to react (Arrhenius).

✗ Stirrer speed

For a true homogeneous reaction, mixing does not change the intrinsic rate.

✗ Product concentration / pressure*

Not in the forward elementary rate law (*pressure only via concentration for gases).

Elementary reaction · Lecture 2-1

The rate law is a power law of concentrations

$$a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightarrow c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D} \qquad r_1 = k_r\, C_A^{\,a}\, C_B^{\,b}$$
$k_r$ = reaction rate constanttemperature-dependent (next slide)
$a + b$ = overall order of reactionfor elementary steps, $a,b$ = stoichiometric coefficients
$[\,r_1\,] = \dfrac{\text{mol}}{\text{m}^3\cdot\text{s}}$rate is intensive — per unit volume

Temperature dependence

Arrhenius equation — how $T$ enters the rate

$$k(T) = A\, e^{-E_a/RT} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k(T) = k(T_0)\, e^{\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_0}-\frac{1}{T}\right)}$$
$A$ = frequency (pre-exponential) factorcollision frequency & orientation
$E_a$ = activation energy (kJ/mol)energy barrier the collision must clear
$R$ = gas constant, $8.314$ J/(mol·K)two-point form avoids needing $A$

Interactive — the linearized Arrhenius plot

Why $\ln k$ vs $1/T$ is a straight line

Take logs: $\ln k = \ln A - \dfrac{E_a}{R}\cdot\dfrac{1}{T}$  — slope $=-E_a/R$, intercept $=\ln A$.
slope $=-E_a/R=$ K  ·  a steeper slope ⇒ larger $E_a$

Section summary · Lecture 2-1

What is the rate expression for an elementary reaction?

Elementary = one step, so reaction order = stoichiometric coefficients: $r = k_r C_A^{a}C_B^{b}$.
Rate depends on concentration and temperature — not on stirring, for a true homogeneous reaction.
Temperature enters only through $k$:  $k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}$.
Linearise to get the constants: $\ln k$ vs $1/T$ is a straight line of slope $-E_a/R$, intercept $\ln A$.

Casework · Lecture 2-1 · exam-style

Find $E_a$ and $A$ from two measurements

A liquid-phase elementary reaction has $k = 1.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at 300 K and $k = 8.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at 320 K. Find $E_a$, then predict $k$ at 340 K.
$\ln\dfrac{k_2}{k_1}=\dfrac{E_a}{R}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_1}-\dfrac{1}{T_2}\right)$  ⇒  $\ln 8 = \dfrac{E_a}{8.314}\left(\dfrac{1}{300}-\dfrac{1}{320}\right)$two-point Arrhenius
$2.079 = \dfrac{E_a}{8.314}(2.083\times10^{-4}) \Rightarrow E_a \approx 83.0$ kJ/molsolve for the barrier
At 340 K: $\ln\dfrac{k_3}{k_2}=\dfrac{83000}{8.314}\left(\dfrac{1}{320}-\dfrac{1}{340}\right)=1.835 \Rightarrow k_3 \approx 5.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$extrapolate

Each 20 K step multiplies $k$ by about 6–8 — the steep temperature sensitivity of an 83 kJ/mol barrier.

How do we obtain the rate expression for a non-elementary reaction?

Definition

A non-elementary reaction is a sum of hidden steps

Apparent reaction

The overall equation is really many elementary sub-steps added together — the visible reaction is only the net result.

Chemical path

Adsorption, surface reaction, desorption — the molecular events that consume and form species.

Physical path

External & internal mass transfer, heat and momentum transfer that feed the chemical steps.

What the rate law looks like

Do not expect a clean power law

The measured rate is the sum of the rates of many sub-steps combined.
The form need not be a power law — it is often a ratio of concentration terms.
Even if it looks like a power law, the exponent (order) need not be an integer — e.g. order $\tfrac{3}{2}$ appears in Lecture 2-3.

Why we bother

Target: a rate in measurable variables

Write the rate using quantities we can actually measure — usually the limiting reactant or the primary product concentration.
$$\text{Fluid phase: } r = f(T,\,C_{\text{limiting}}) \qquad \text{Solid catalytic: } r = f(T,\,C_{\text{limiting, bulk}})$$
The expression then feeds reactor design through the mass & energy balances.

The workflow

How to determine a rate expression

Step 2, in detail

Two tools to collapse the mechanism

1 · Rate-limiting step (RLS)

The slowest step controls the overall rate. Any non-limiting reversible step is taken to be at equilibrium ($r=0$).

2 · Pseudo-steady-state (PSSH)

Reactive intermediates — radicals, surface / enzyme complexes — are assumed to have net rate $\approx 0$: they form and vanish as fast as they appear.

Both tools remove the unmeasurable intermediates, leaving a rate in measurable concentrations.

Interactive — find the bottleneck

Why the slowest step controls the whole rate

Steps in series behave like resistances: $\dfrac{1}{k_{ov}}=\sum\dfrac{1}{k_i}$. Speeding up a fast step barely helps.

Interactive — when is PSSH allowed?

The intermediate must stay small and flat

$\mathrm{A}\xrightarrow{k_1}\mathrm{I}\xrightarrow{k_2}\mathrm{P}$ — PSSH sets $\dfrac{dC_I}{dt}\approx 0$.

Section summary · Lecture 2-2

How do we obtain a non-elementary rate expression?

An apparent reaction is the sum of hidden elementary steps; the rate law need not be a power law.
Workflow: propose a mechanism → derive the rate → fit the data; if it does not fit, revise the mechanism.
Tool 1 — the rate-limiting step carries most of the resistance; speeding up a fast step changes little.
Tool 2 — PSSH removes unmeasurable intermediates, valid only when they never accumulate.

Casework · Lecture 2-2 · exam-style

Reject a mechanism that cannot fit the data

Experiments on $\mathrm{A}+\mathrm{B}\to\mathrm{P}$ give a rate that is first order in A but becomes zero order in B at high $C_B$. A student proposes the elementary rate $-r_A = kC_AC_B$. Is the proposal acceptable?
No. $kC_AC_B$ is first order in B at every concentration — it can never saturate, so it contradicts the observed zero-order limit.
A mechanism with a saturating B-binding step gives  $-r_A = \dfrac{kC_AC_B}{1+K_BC_B}$consistent with both limits
Low $C_B$: $\;-r_A \approx kC_AC_B$  ·  High $C_B$: $\;-r_A \approx (k/K_B)C_A$first order → zero order in B

Step 3 of the workflow did its job: the data rejected the simple mechanism and forced a better one.

Rate expression from multiple elementary steps — a chain reaction

Worked system · pyrolysis of acetaldehyde

An apparent reaction hides four elementary steps

$$\mathrm{CH_3CHO} \xrightarrow{\;k_{\text{eff}}\;} \mathrm{CH_4 + CO}$$
StepElementary reactionRate const.
Initiation$\mathrm{CH_3CHO} \xrightarrow{\;k_a\;} \mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet} + CHO^{\bullet}}$$k_a$
Propagation (RLS)$\mathrm{CH_3CHO + CH_3^{\bullet}} \xrightarrow{\;k_b\;} \mathrm{CH_4 + CH_3CO^{\bullet}}$$k_b$
Propagation$\mathrm{CH_3CO^{\bullet}} \;\underset{k_c'}{\overset{k_c}{\rightleftharpoons}}\; \mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet} + CO}$$k_c,\,k_c'$
Termination$\mathrm{2\,CH_3^{\bullet}} \xrightarrow{\;k_d\;} \mathrm{C_2H_6}$$k_d$

Reversible steps use $\rightleftharpoons$; a reversible non-limiting step is taken at equilibrium ($r=0$), while the irreversible RLS ($\rightarrow$) controls the overall rate.

Applying PSSH to the radicals

Sum the reaction rates of each radical to zero

Radicals $\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}},\ \mathrm{CHO^{\bullet}},\ \mathrm{CH_3CO^{\bullet}}$ are intermediates ⇒ each net rate $=0$.  Let $C_A \equiv C_{\mathrm{CH_3CHO}}$.
$$r_{\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}}} = \underbrace{k_a C_A}_{\text{initiation}} - \underbrace{k_b C_A C_{\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}}}}_{\text{step b}} + \underbrace{k_c C_{\mathrm{CH_3CO^{\bullet}}}}_{\text{step c}} - \underbrace{2k_d C_{\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}}}^{2}}_{\text{termination}} = 0$$
Propagation terms (b, c) cancel  ⇒  $k_a C_A = 2k_d C_{\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}}}^{2}$initiation balances termination
$\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}}} = \left(\tfrac{k_a}{2k_d}\,C_A\right)^{1/2}$solve for the radical concentration

The result · Lecture 2-3

Substitute the radical back into the RLS

From the RLS (step b):  $-r_{\mathrm{CH_3CHO}} = k_b\,C_A\,C_{\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}}}$rate set by the slow step
Insert $C_{\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}}} = \left(\tfrac{k_a}{2k_d}\,C_A\right)^{1/2}$ from the previous slideeliminate the unmeasurable radical
$$-r_{\mathrm{CH_3CHO}} = k_b\left(\frac{k_a}{2k_d}\right)^{1/2} C_{\mathrm{CH_3CHO}}^{3/2} \;=\; k_{\text{eff}}\, C_{\mathrm{CH_3CHO}}^{3/2}$$
Overall order $\tfrac{3}{2}$ — non-integer — even though every hidden step is elementary.

Section summary · Lecture 2-3

Rate expression from multiple elementary steps

A chain mechanism has four roles: initiation, propagation (×2), termination.
PSSH on the radicals: initiation balances termination ⇒ $C_{\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}}}\propto C_A^{1/2}$.
Substituting into the RLS gives $-r_A=k_{\mathrm{eff}}C_A^{3/2}$ — a non-integer order from elementary steps.
$k_{\mathrm{eff}}=k_b\sqrt{k_a/2k_d}$ lumps three constants into one measurable parameter.

What is the rate expression for an equilibrium (reversible) reaction?

Worked system · glycerol carbonate synthesis

Net rate = forward − reverse

$$\text{DMC} + \text{Glycerol} \underset{k'_{-1}}{\overset{k'_{1}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Glycerol carbonate} + 2\,\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$$
$$r'_1 = k'_1 C_D C_{Gly} - k'_{-1} C_{GC} C_{Me} = k'_1\!\left(C_D C_{Gly} - \frac{C_{GC} C_{Me}}{K_e}\right)$$

At equilibrium $r'_1 = 0$ — the bracket vanishes, recovering the equilibrium constant.

Temperature dependence of $K_e$

The equilibrium constant is a ratio of rate constants

$$K_e = \frac{k_1}{k_{-1}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad K_e(T) = K_e(T_0)\, e^{\frac{\Delta H^{\circ}_{rxn}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_0}-\frac{1}{T}\right)}$$
$E_1 - E_{-1} = \Delta H_{rxn}$barrier difference = heat of reaction
Exothermic $\Delta H < 0$:  $K_e$ falls as $T$ risesvan't Hoff — heat is a product
Endothermic $\Delta H > 0$:  $K_e$ rises as $T$ risesheat is a reactant

Section summary · Lecture 2-4

Rate expression for an equilibrium reaction

Net rate = forward − reverse: $r=k_1\!\left(C_AC_B-\dfrac{C_CC_D}{K_e}\right)$; it is zero at equilibrium.
$K_e=k_1/k_{-1}$ — the equilibrium constant is a ratio of rate constants.
Barriers set the thermochemistry: $E_1-E_{-1}=\Delta H_{rxn}$.
Van't Hoff: exothermic ⇒ $K_e$ falls with $T$; endothermic ⇒ $K_e$ rises.

Casework · Lecture 2-4 · exam-style

Choosing the operating temperature

An exothermic reversible reaction ($\Delta H_{rxn}=-60$ kJ/mol) has $K_e=50$ at 300 K. Find $K_e$ at 450 K and state the design consequence.
$K_e(T)=K_e(T_0)\exp\!\left[\dfrac{\Delta H}{R}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_0}-\dfrac{1}{T}\right)\right]$van't Hoff
$\dfrac{1}{300}-\dfrac{1}{450}=1.111\times10^{-3}$,  $\dfrac{-60000}{8.314}=-7217$ ⇒ exponent $=-8.02$evaluate
$K_e(450)=50\,e^{-8.02}\approx 1.6\times10^{-2}$a fall of ~3000×
Design consequence: heating buys rate but destroys equilibrium conversion. Run hot early (fast kinetics), then cool to push conversion — the staged adiabatic bed with inter-stage cooling.

What is the rate expression for an enzymatic reaction?

Interactive — drag the progress slider

Substrate binds, reacts, product leaves

Step 1 — free enzyme and substrate
$\mathrm{E} + \mathrm{S}$

Two elementary steps

Adsorption then reaction

$\mathrm{E + S} \underset{k_2}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}} \mathrm{E{\cdot}S}$1 · adsorption — enzyme–substrate complex forms
$\mathrm{E{\cdot}S} \xrightarrow{\;k_3\;} \mathrm{E + P}$2 · reaction — product releases, enzyme recovered
Total enzyme is conserved:  $C_{E_T} = C_E + C_{E{\cdot}S}$  — we can measure $C_{E_T}$, $C_S$, $C_P$, but not the complex.

Interactive — the enzyme balance

What is $C_{E_T}$, and what is it made of?

Enzyme is a catalyst — it is never consumed, so every molecule you charged is still there, in one of only two forms.
$C_E$ and $C_{E{\cdot}S}$ cannot be measured — but $C_{E_T}$ is what you weighed into the reactor.
$C_{E_T} = \underbrace{C_E}_{\text{free}} + \underbrace{C_{E{\cdot}S}}_{\text{bound}}$
only the bound form makes product:  $r_P = k_3 C_{E{\cdot}S}$

PSSH + enzyme balance → Michaelis–Menten

Eliminate $C_E$ and $C_{E\cdot S}$ to reach a measurable rate

$$\mathrm{E + S}\;\underset{k_2}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\mathrm{E{\cdot}S}\;\xrightarrow{\;k_3\;}\;\mathrm{E + P}$$
①  $k_1 C_E C_S - (k_2+k_3)C_{E{\cdot}S} = 0$PSSH on the complex
②  $C_{E{\cdot}S} = \dfrac{C_E C_S}{K_M}$,  $K_M \equiv \dfrac{k_2+k_3}{k_1}$define $K_M$
③  $C_E = C_{E_T} - C_{E{\cdot}S}$  → insert into ②enzyme balance removes $C_E$
④  $C_{E{\cdot}S}(K_M + C_S) = C_{E_T}C_S \Rightarrow C_{E{\cdot}S} = \dfrac{C_{E_T}C_S}{K_M + C_S}$solve for the complex
$$r_P = k_3 C_{E{\cdot}S} = \frac{V_{max}\,C_S}{K_M + C_S}, \qquad V_{max}\equiv k_3 C_{E_T}$$

Saturation kinetics: $\;r_P = \dfrac{V_{max} C_S}{K_M + C_S}$

At $C_S = K_M$ the rate is exactly $\tfrac12 V_{max}$ (dashed lines). Large $C_S$ → rate saturates at $V_{max}$.
half-saturation at $C_S=K_M=$

Interactive — linearize to find the constants

Lineweaver–Burk: $\;\dfrac{1}{r_P} = \dfrac{K_M}{V_{max}}\dfrac{1}{C_S} + \dfrac{1}{V_{max}}$

A double-reciprocal plot is a straight line: slope $=K_M/V_{max}$, y-intercept $=1/V_{max}$, x-intercept $=-1/K_M$.

Worked example · deriving an inhibited rate

Example — a third enzyme form appears

An inhibitor $I$ also binds the enzyme. Derive $r_P$ in measurable quantities ($C_{E_T}$, $C_S$, $C_I$).
(1)  $\mathrm{E + S} \underset{k_2}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}} \mathrm{E{\cdot}S}$productive binding, $K_M=\frac{k_2+k_3}{k_1}$
(2)  $\mathrm{E{\cdot}S} \xrightarrow{\;k_3\;} \mathrm{E + P}$only this step makes product
(3)  $\mathrm{E + I} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{E{\cdot}I}$  (dead end)$K_I = \dfrac{C_E C_I}{C_{E{\cdot}I}}$
Now the enzyme exists in three forms, so the balance becomes  $C_{E_T} = C_E + C_{E{\cdot}S} + C_{E{\cdot}I}$

Worked example · solution

The inhibitor inflates the $K_M$ term

①  $C_{E{\cdot}S} = \dfrac{C_E C_S}{K_M}$  ·  $C_{E{\cdot}I} = \dfrac{C_E C_I}{K_I}$PSSH / equilibrium on each complex
②  $C_{E_T} = C_E\left(1 + \dfrac{C_S}{K_M} + \dfrac{C_I}{K_I}\right)$factor $C_E$ out of the balance
③  $C_{E{\cdot}S} = \dfrac{C_{E_T}\,C_S/K_M}{1 + C_S/K_M + C_I/K_I}$eliminate the unmeasurable $C_E$
$$r_P = k_3C_{E{\cdot}S} = \frac{V_{max}C_S}{C_S + K_M\left(1+\dfrac{C_I}{K_I}\right)}$$

Multiply through by $K_M$: only the $K_M$ term is inflated ⇒ apparent $K_M$ rises, $V_{max}$ is untouched — competitive inhibition.

Section summary · Lecture 2-5

Rate expression for an enzymatic reaction

Enzyme is conserved: $C_{E_T}=C_E+C_{E\cdot S}$ — only the bound form makes product.
PSSH + balance ⇒ $r_P=\dfrac{V_{max}C_S}{K_M+C_S}$, with $V_{max}=k_3C_{E_T}$, $K_M=\dfrac{k_2+k_3}{k_1}$.
Lineweaver–Burk linearises it: slope $K_M/V_{max}$, y-int $1/V_{max}$, x-int $-1/K_M$.
Inhibitors add a term in the denominator: competitive ⇒ $K_M\!\uparrow$; non-competitive ⇒ $V_{max}\!\downarrow$.

Rate expression for cell growth

Worked example · deriving Andrews (Haldane)

Example — a second substrate blocks the site

At high $S$ a second substrate molecule binds the complex and kills it. Derive $\mu(S)$.
(1)  $\mathrm{E + S} \underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}} \mathrm{ES}$$K_S \equiv \frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1}$
(2)  $\mathrm{ES} \xrightarrow{\;k_2\;} \mathrm{E} + \text{growth precursors}$the productive step
(3)  $\mathrm{ES + S} \underset{k_{-3}}{\overset{k_3}{\rightleftharpoons}} \mathrm{ESS}$  (dead end)$K_I \equiv \frac{k_{-3}}{k_3}$
SSA on ESS:  $k_3[\mathrm{ES}]S = k_{-3}[\mathrm{ESS}] \Rightarrow [\mathrm{ESS}] = \dfrac{[\mathrm{ES}]\,S}{K_I}$these terms then cancel in the ES balance
SSA on ES therefore reduces to  $[\mathrm{ES}] = \dfrac{[\mathrm{E}]\,S}{K_S}$

Worked example · solution

The dead-end complex adds the $S^2$ term

①  $[\mathrm{E}_T] = [\mathrm{E}] + [\mathrm{ES}] + [\mathrm{ESS}]$now three enzyme forms
②  $[\mathrm{E}_T] = [\mathrm{E}]\left(1 + \dfrac{S}{K_S} + \dfrac{S^2}{K_S K_I}\right)$factor out $[\mathrm{E}]$
③  $[\mathrm{ES}] = \dfrac{[\mathrm{E}_T]\,S}{K_S + S + S^2/K_I}$multiply through by $K_S$
$$\mu(S) = \frac{k_2[\mathrm{E}_T]}{X}\cdot\frac{S}{K_S + S + \frac{S^2}{K_I}} = \mu_{max}\frac{S}{K_S + S + \frac{S^2}{K_I}}$$

Checks: $S \ll K_S \Rightarrow \mu \propto S$  ·  $S \gg \sqrt{K_S K_I} \Rightarrow \mu \to 0$. Drop the $S^2$ term and Monod is recovered.

Section summary · Lecture 2-6

Rate expression for a microorganism reaction

Batch culture runs through lag → log → stationary → death; Monod applies in the log phase.
Monod: $r_g=\mu C_C$, $\mu=\mu_{max}\dfrac{C_S}{K_S+C_S}$ — same algebra as Michaelis–Menten.
Andrews (Haldane) adds $S^2/K_I$: $\mu$ peaks at $S^{*}=\sqrt{K_SK_I}$, then declines.
The generalized model multiplies factors for substrate, oxygen and product inhibition.

Key Takeaways

1

Read the order off the stoichiometry only for an elementary step; anything else needs a mechanism.

2

Derive every non-elementary rate the same way: mechanism → PSSH/RLS → catalyst balance → eliminate the unmeasurable species.

3

Expect non-integer orders and saturating ratios — $3/2$-order chains, Michaelis–Menten, and Monod all come from this recipe.

4

Separate kinetics from thermodynamics: heating always raises $k$, but for an exothermic reaction it lowers $K_e$.

5

Check an inhibition signature before acting: $K_M\!\uparrow$ means competitive, $V_{max}\!\downarrow$ means non-competitive.
