What is the rate expression for a reaction controlled by an elementary single reaction?

Definition

What is an elementary reaction?

Single step

Reactants become products directly — no detectable intermediate.

Collision-driven

Two or three molecules must meet for one event.

Order = molecularity

Read the exponents straight off the balanced equation.

Homogeneous and non-catalytic: one fluid phase, no surface.

Before we write an equation

What does the reaction rate depend on?

One step, one collision event — so only what changes that event can change the rate.

✓ Concentration

More reactant, more collisions per second.

✓ Temperature

Sets what fraction of those collisions is energetic enough.

Before we write an equation

… and what it does not depend on

✗ Stirrer speed

Mixing moves molecules; it does not make a collision react.

✗ Product concentration

Absent from the forward rate law of a single elementary step.

Gas pressure enters only through concentration, never on its own.

Section summary · Lecture 2-1

What is the rate expression for an elementary reaction?

Elementary = one step, so reaction order = stoichiometric coefficients: $r = k_r C_A^{a}C_B^{b}$.
Rate depends on concentration and temperature — not on stirring, for a true homogeneous reaction.
Temperature enters only through $k$:  $k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}$.
Linearise to get the constants: $\ln k$ vs $1/T$ is a straight line of slope $-E_a/R$, intercept $\ln A$.

How do we obtain the rate expression for a non-elementary reaction?

Definition

A non-elementary reaction is a sum of hidden steps

Apparent reaction

What you write down is only the net result.

Chemical path

Adsorption, surface reaction, desorption.

Physical path

Mass, heat and momentum transfer feeding those steps.

The hidden steps are elementary; their sum is not.

What the rate law looks like

Do not expect a clean power law

Many hidden steps add up, so the shape of the rate law is no longer guaranteed.

The measured rate combines every sub-step at once.
Its form is often a ratio, not a product.
Orders need not be integers — $\tfrac{3}{2}$ appears in Lecture 2-3.

Why we bother

Target: a rate in measurable variables

Write the rate using quantities we can actually measure — usually the limiting reactant or the primary product concentration.
$$\text{Fluid phase: } r = f(T,\,C_{\text{limiting}}) \qquad \text{Solid catalytic: } r = f(T,\,C_{\text{limiting, bulk}})$$
The expression then feeds reactor design through the mass & energy balances.

$-r_A$ = consumption rate; $C_i$ = concentrations you can sample.

The workflow

How to determine a rate expression

Step 2, in detail

Tool 1 — the rate-limiting steptool 1 of 2

The mechanism has more steps than we can measure. This tool ignores most of them.

The slowest step carries the resistance and sets the overall rate.
Every faster reversible step is then taken to sit at equilibrium.

Fails when two steps run at comparable rates — then neither limits.

Interactive — find the bottleneck

Why the slowest step controls the whole rate

Steps in series behave like resistances: $\dfrac{1}{k_{ov}}=\sum\dfrac{1}{k_i}$. Speeding up a fast step barely helps.

What it proves: Speeding anything but the slowest step barely moves the rate.

Step 2, in detail

Tool 2 — pseudo-steady-state hypothesistool 2 of 2

A rate-limiting step needs one slow step. When none dominates, use the intermediates instead.

A reactive intermediate is consumed as fast as it forms — the pseudo-steady-state hypothesis (PSSH).
Setting its net rate to zero turns a differential equation into algebra.

Fails once the intermediate accumulates to a measurable fraction.

Interactive — when is PSSH allowed?

The intermediate must stay small and flat

$\mathrm{A}\xrightarrow{k_1}\mathrm{I}\xrightarrow{k_2}\mathrm{P}$ — PSSH sets $\dfrac{dC_I}{dt}\approx 0$.

What it proves: PSSH holds only while the intermediate stays at trace level.

Casework · Lecture 2-2 · exam-style

Reject a mechanism that cannot fit the data

Experiments on $\mathrm{A}+\mathrm{B}\to\mathrm{P}$ give a rate that is first order in A but becomes zero order in B at high $C_B$. A student proposes the elementary rate $-r_A = kC_AC_B$. Is the proposal acceptable?
No. $kC_AC_B$ is first order in B at every concentration — it can never saturate, so it contradicts the observed zero-order limit.
A mechanism with a saturating B-binding step gives  $-r_A = \dfrac{kC_AC_B}{1+K_BC_B}$consistent with both limits
Low $C_B$: $\;-r_A \approx kC_AC_B$  ·  High $C_B$: $\;-r_A \approx (k/K_B)C_A$first order → zero order in B

Step 3 did its job: the data rejected the simple mechanism.

What is the rate expression for an equilibrium (reversible) reaction?

Worked system · glycerol carbonate synthesis

Net rate = forward − reverse

Every rate so far ran one way. Real reactions also run backwards, and that changes the ceiling.

$$\text{DMC} + \text{Glycerol} \underset{k'_{-1}}{\overset{k'_{1}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Glycerol carbonate} + 2\,\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$$
$$r'_1 = k'_1 C_D C_{Gly} - k'_{-1} C_{GC} C_{Me} = k'_1\!\left(C_D C_{Gly} - \frac{C_{GC} C_{Me}}{K_e}\right)$$

At equilibrium $r'_1 = 0$ — the bracket vanishes, recovering the equilibrium constant.

Temperature dependence of $K_e$

The equilibrium constant is a ratio of rate constants

$$K_e = \frac{k_1}{k_{-1}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad K_e(T) = K_e(T_0)\, e^{\frac{\Delta H^{\circ}_{rxn}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_0}-\frac{1}{T}\right)}$$
$E_1 - E_{-1} = \Delta H_{rxn}$barrier difference = heat of reaction
Exothermic $\Delta H < 0$:  $K_e$ falls as $T$ risesvan't Hoff — heat is a product
Endothermic $\Delta H > 0$:  $K_e$ rises as $T$ risesheat is a reactant

$K_e$ = equilibrium constant; $\Delta H_{rxn}$ = heat of reaction, J/mol.

Two elementary steps

Adsorption then reaction

PSSH + enzyme balance → Michaelis–Menten

Eliminate $C_E$ and $C_{E\cdot S}$ to reach a measurable rate

$$\mathrm{E + S}\;\underset{k_2}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\mathrm{E{\cdot}S}\;\xrightarrow{\;k_3\;}\;\mathrm{E + P}$$
①  $k_1 C_E C_S - (k_2+k_3)C_{E{\cdot}S} = 0$PSSH on the complex
②  $C_{E{\cdot}S} = \dfrac{C_E C_S}{K_M}$,  $K_M \equiv \dfrac{k_2+k_3}{k_1}$define $K_M$
③  $C_E = C_{E_T} - C_{E{\cdot}S}$  → insert into ②enzyme balance removes $C_E$
④  $C_{E{\cdot}S}(K_M + C_S) = C_{E_T}C_S \Rightarrow C_{E{\cdot}S} = \dfrac{C_{E_T}C_S}{K_M + C_S}$solve for the complex
$$r_P = k_3 C_{E{\cdot}S} = \frac{V_{max}\,C_S}{K_M + C_S}, \qquad V_{max}\equiv k_3 C_{E_T}$$

Called the Michaelis–Menten enzyme kinetics equation; $K_M$ = Michaelis constant, mol/dm³.

Worked example · Lecture 2-5

Enzymatic reaction with an inhibitor — the full mechanism

①  $\mathrm{E}+\mathrm{S}\;\underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\mathrm{E{\cdot}S}$
②  $\mathrm{E}+\mathrm{I}\;\underset{k_{-2}}{\overset{k_2}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\mathrm{E{\cdot}I}$
③  $\mathrm{E{\cdot}S}+\mathrm{I}\;\underset{k_{-3}}{\overset{k_3}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\mathrm{E{\cdot}I{\cdot}S}$
④  $\mathrm{E{\cdot}I}+\mathrm{S}\;\underset{k_{-4}}{\overset{k_4}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\mathrm{E{\cdot}I{\cdot}S}$
⑤  $\mathrm{E{\cdot}S}\;\xrightarrow{\;k_{r1}\;}\;\mathrm{E}+\mathrm{P}$
⑥  $\mathrm{E{\cdot}I{\cdot}S}\;\xrightarrow{\;k_{r2}\;}\;\mathrm{E{\cdot}I}+\mathrm{P}$
  • ①② — S and I compete for the same free enzyme, giving two binary complexes E·S and E·I.
  • ③④ — either binary complex can pick up the missing partner, so the ternary complex E·I·S has two routes in.
  • ⑤⑥ — product leaves from E·S, and from E·I·S too unless the ternary complex is dead ($k_{r2}=0$, step ⑥ disappears).

Before any algebra · Lecture 2-5

Three assumptionssetup 1 of 2

1 · Steps ①–④ are fast — all four binding steps sit at equilibrium, so each becomes a ratio.
2 · $k_{r1}=k_{r2}$ — the ternary complex turns over as fast as E·S.
3 · Enzyme is conserved — $C_{E_T}=C_E+C_{E\cdot S}+C_{E\cdot I}+C_{E\cdot I\cdot S}$.

Set $k_{r2}=0$ later and the same algebra gives the dead-end case.

④  put $C_H$ back into the $\mathrm{H^{\bullet}}$ equation:  $k_3C_{R}^2-k_1C_AC_{R}-\dfrac{k_1k_4}{k_5}C_A^2=0$a quadratic in $C_R$

Example I · step 3 of 3

Solve, then let the initiation be slow

$$C_{R}=\left\{\frac{k_1}{2k_3}\pm\left[\left(\frac{k_1}{2k_3}\right)^{2}+\frac{k_1k_4}{k_3k_5}\right]^{1/2}\right\}C_A$$

Only the $+$ root is physical — a concentration cannot be negative.

Initiation is by far the slowest step, $k_1\ll k_3$. What does the root become, and what is $r=k_3C_R$?
$\left(\dfrac{k_1}{2k_3}\right)^{2}\ll\dfrac{k_1k_4}{k_3k_5}\;\Rightarrow\;C_{R}\simeq\left(\dfrac{k_1k_4}{k_3k_5}\right)^{1/2}C_A$drop the small square
$$r=k_3C_{R}=\left[\frac{k_1k_3k_4}{k_5}\right]^{1/2}C_A$$

First order — exactly what the experiment reported, so the mechanism survives the test.

Example I · the lesson

Square roots are the fingerprint of a chainlesson 1 of 2

The algebra is done. Now read why the answer had to look like that.

Termination is bimolecular, so the radical pool follows $\sqrt{\text{initiation}/\text{termination}}$.
Every constant from those two steps therefore enters at the $1/2$ power.

Example II · problem

Gas-phase oxidation of ethylene to ethylene oxide

$$\mathrm{C_2H_4}+\tfrac12\,\mathrm{O_2}\rightarrow\mathrm{C_2H_4O}$$
Two radicals this time: $\mathrm{O^{\bullet}}$ and $\mathrm{C_2H_4O^{\bullet}}$. Find the order in each reactant.
Shorthand:  $x\equiv C_{\mathrm{O^{\bullet}}}$,  $y\equiv C_{\mathrm{C_2H_4O^{\bullet}}}$.
StepElementary reaction
initiation$\mathrm{O_2}\xrightarrow{\;k_i\;}2\,\mathrm{O^{\bullet}}$
propagation$\mathrm{O^{\bullet}}+\mathrm{C_2H_4}\xrightarrow{\;k_{p2}\;}\mathrm{C_2H_4O^{\bullet}}$

$x$, $y$ = the two radical concentrations; $k_t$ = termination constant.

Example II · step 1 of 3

SSA on the carbon radical links $y$ to $x$

$\dfrac{dy}{dt}=k_{p2}\,x\,C_{\mathrm{C_2H_4}}-k_{p1}\,y\,C_{\mathrm{O_2}}\approx0$made in the 2nd step, eaten in the 3rd
$$y=\frac{k_{p2}}{k_{p1}}\cdot\frac{x\,C_{\mathrm{C_2H_4}}}{C_{\mathrm{O_2}}}$$
Read it physically: the more oxygen there is, the faster $\mathrm{C_2H_4O^{\bullet}}$ is swept away, so the less of it survives at steady state.
Keep this relation as it is — in the next step it makes two whole terms disappear.

Example II · step 2 of 3

SSA on $\mathrm{O^{\bullet}}$: the propagation terms cancel

sources:  $+2k_iC_{\mathrm{O_2}}$  (initiation)  $+\,k_{p1}\,y\,C_{\mathrm{O_2}}$  (regenerated)where $\mathrm{O^{\bullet}}$ comes from
sinks:  $-k_{p2}\,x\,C_{\mathrm{C_2H_4}}$  (propagation)  $-\,2k_tx^{2}$  (termination)where it goes
$$2k_iC_{\mathrm{O_2}}+\underbrace{k_{p1}yC_{\mathrm{O_2}}-k_{p2}xC_{\mathrm{C_2H_4}}}_{=\;0\;\text{ by step 1}}-\,2k_tx^{2}=0$$
$$x=\left(\frac{k_i\,C_{\mathrm{O_2}}}{k_t}\right)^{1/2}$$

Again initiation = termination. The propagation constants never reach the radical pool.

Example II · step 3 of 3

The product rate, and where the half order comes from

$r_{\mathrm{EO}}=k_{p1}\,y\,C_{\mathrm{O_2}}=k_{p2}\,x\,C_{\mathrm{C_2H_4}}$one epoxide per turn of the chain
$$r_{\mathrm{EO}}=k_{p2}\left(\frac{k_i}{k_t}\right)^{1/2}C_{\mathrm{C_2H_4}}\,C_{\mathrm{O_2}}^{1/2}$$

Order 1 in $\mathrm{C_2H_4}$

Ethylene appears once, in the step that consumes the carrier.

Order $\tfrac12$ in $\mathrm{O_2}$

Oxygen starts the chain, and the carrier is killed two at a time — hence the square root.

The $\tfrac12$ is a coincidence of the chain, not a reading of the $\tfrac12\,\mathrm{O_2}$ in the balanced equation.

Interactive — change how the chain dies

Termination sets the observed order in $\mathrm{O_2}$

Bimolecular death → $x\propto C_{\mathrm{O_2}}^{1/2}$. Kill radicals on the wall instead (first order) and the same chain becomes first order in oxygen.

What it proves: Termination, not stoichiometry, fixes the order in oxygen.

Example III · problem

Enzymatic reaction with two substrates — lactate dehydrogenase

$$\mathrm{Lactate}+\mathrm{NAD^{+}}\;\underset{\mathrm{LDH}}{\rightleftharpoons}\;\mathrm{Pyruvate}+\mathrm{NADH}+\mathrm{H^{+}}$$

Example III · step 3 of 3

The Ordered Bi–Bi rate law

$$v=\frac{V_{max}\,C_{S_1}C_{S_2}}{K_{iA}K_{mB}+K_{mB}C_{S_1}+K_{mA}C_{S_2}+C_{S_1}C_{S_2}}$$
$C_{S_1},C_{S_2}$ both small$v\simeq\dfrac{V_{max}}{K_{iA}K_{mB}}C_{S_1}C_{S_2}$ — second order overall
$C_{S_1}$ saturating$v\simeq\dfrac{V_{max}C_{S_2}}{K_{mB}+C_{S_2}}$ — plain Michaelis–Menten in $\mathrm{NAD^{+}}$
both saturating$v\to V_{max}$ — every enzyme molecule is a loaded ternary complex
If the first binding is fast enough to equilibrate ($k_{cat}\ll k_{-1}$, i.e. $K_{mA}\ll K_{iA}$) the third denominator term drops and the shorter three-term form in the handout is recovered.

Example IV · problem

Microorganism growth on a single substrate

$$\mathrm{S\;(substrate)}\xrightarrow{\;\text{micro-organism}\;}\mathrm{X\;(biomass)}$$
Cells are not molecules. How do we get a rate law for something that grows?
Monod's idea: growth is limited by substrate binding to a transport site / enzyme inside the cell — so borrow Michaelis–Menten, then tie the site count to the biomass.
$\mathrm{E}+\mathrm{S}\underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}}\mathrm{E{\cdot}S}$uptake / binding
$\mathrm{E{\cdot}S}\xrightarrow{\;k_2\;}\mathrm{E}+\text{growth precursors}$conversion
$\mathrm{E}$ = an active site inside the cell, so the total site count $C_{E_T}\propto C_X$, the biomass concentration.

$\rho$ = active sites per unit biomass; $k_2$ = conversion constant.

Example IV · step 1 of 2

SSA plus a site balance — identical algebra to Michaelis–Menten

$\dfrac{dC_{E\cdot S}}{dt}=k_1C_EC_S-k_{-1}C_{E\cdot S}-k_2C_{E\cdot S}\approx0$SSA on the loaded site
$C_{E\cdot S}=\dfrac{C_EC_S}{K_S},\qquad K_S\equiv\dfrac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1}$defines the half-saturation constant
$C_{E_T}=C_E+C_{E\cdot S}\;\Rightarrow\;C_{E\cdot S}=\dfrac{C_{E_T}C_S}{K_S+C_S}$site balance removes $C_E$
$$\text{precursor rate}=k_2C_{E\cdot S}=\frac{k_2C_{E_T}C_S}{K_S+C_S}$$

Example IV · step 2 of 2

Tie the site count to the biomass and Monod appears

$\mu\equiv\dfrac{1}{C_X}\dfrac{dC_X}{dt}$,  and  $C_{E_T}=\rho\,C_X$specific growth rate; sites per unit biomass
$$\mu=\mu_{max}\frac{C_S}{K_S+C_S},\qquad \mu_{max}=k_2\,\rho$$
$C_S\ll K_S$$\mu\propto C_S$ — first order, substrate-starved
$C_S\gg K_S$$\mu\to\mu_{max}$ — zero order, every site is busy
For a batch design this closes with two mass balances:  $\dfrac{dC_X}{dt}=\mu C_X$  and  $\dfrac{dC_S}{dt}=-\dfrac{1}{Y_{X/S}}\dfrac{dC_X}{dt}$.

Example IV · putting it together

The generalized Monod-type model

$$\mu(C_S,C_{\mathrm{O_2}},C_P)=\frac{\mu_{max}}{1+\dfrac{C_P}{K_{I,P}}}\cdot\frac{C_S}{K_S+C_S+\dfrac{C_S^{2}}{K_I}}\cdot\frac{C_{\mathrm{O_2}}}{K_O+C_{\mathrm{O_2}}}$$
$$r_1=\mu_1\,C_{X_1}$$

Rate is growth rate × biomass: $\mu$ how fast each cell works, $C_X$ how many.

This is the form used in bioprocess scale-up: fit $\mu_{max},K_S,K_I,K_O,K_{I,P}$ from lab runs, then trust it only inside the range you fitted.

Take-home: an inhibited chain

StepElementary reaction
initiation$\mathrm{M}\xrightarrow{\;k_i\;}2\,\mathrm{R^{\bullet}}$
propagation$\mathrm{R^{\bullet}}+\mathrm{M}\xrightarrow{\;k_p\;}\mathrm{P}+\mathrm{R^{\bullet}}$
inhibition$\mathrm{R^{\bullet}}+\mathrm{Z}\xrightarrow{\;k_z\;}\text{dead}$  (scavenger $\mathrm{Z}$, present in trace)

Termination is now first order in $\mathrm{R^{\bullet}}$, so no square root appears: the order moved because the termination step changed, exactly as in Example II.
