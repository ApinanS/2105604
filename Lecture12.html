Which model, which vessel

Pick the model by how the vessel departs from plug flow

Small deviation

Dispersion or tanks-in-series: this lecture. Turbulent pipes, long laminar tubes, packed beds, kilns, channels.

Laminar, short tube

The parabolic velocity profile dominates: convection model, where each streamline keeps its own speed and diffusion is ignored.

Gross faults

Dead water, bypass, recycle: compartment models, covered separately.

The two small-deviation models are roughly equivalent. Use the one you find comfortable.

Dispersed plug flow

Plug flow, plus a random back-and-forth shuffle

  • Eddies, velocity differences and diffusion shuffle fluid along the axis.
  • The shuffle has the same intensity everywhere in the vessel.
  • No stagnant pockets, no bypass.

Dispersed plug flow

Each cross-section is well mixed; $D$ acts only along the axis

  • Radial (across the tube): assumed well mixed — $C$ is the same over the whole cross-section.
  • Axial (along the tube): mixing is not complete — its strength is the axial dispersion coefficient $D$.

Lecture 12-1

What section 12-1 settled

  • The dispersion model is plug flow plus a uniform, diffusion-like axial shuffle of strength $D$.
  • One group, $D/uL$, sets the spread: 0 is plug flow, ∞ is mixed flow.

The Gaussian curve

Small spread: the E curve is a symmetric bell

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\bar x)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]$$

The normal curve: a bell-shaped curve, symmetric about $\bar x$, width set by $\sigma$.

$x\to\theta,\ \ \bar x\to1,\ \ \sigma^2\to\sigma_\theta^2=2\,\dfrac{D}{uL}$
put the dispersion values in
$$\mathbf E_\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\,(D/uL)}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(1-\theta)^2}{4\,(D/uL)}\right]$$

Same mean, $\theta=1$. Larger $D/uL$: lower peak, wider bell. $\theta=t/\bar t$; $\mathbf E_\theta=\bar t\,\mathbf E$.

Why only small $D/uL$? A bell is symmetric. At $D/uL=0.01$, $\sigma_\theta=0.14$: the pulse barely spreads while it passes the exit. Larger $D/uL$ skews the real curve.

Condition behind E

E is defined for fluid that enters once and leaves once

  • Closed: each element has one age, so the measured curve $=\mathbf E$, $\int_0^\infty \mathbf E\,dt=1$ and $\bar t=V/v$.
  • Open: an element can cross the exit twice, so the measured curve is not $\mathbf E$ and $\bar t>V/v$.
  • Most reactors with small inlet and outlet pipes behave as closed vessels.

The tracer relations of Lecture 11 assume a closed vessel.

Closed or open ends

Open or closed refers to each end: inlet and outlet

  • Each end is judged on its own: the inlet boundary $z=0$ and the outlet boundary $z=1$.
  • Closed end: plug flow in the pipe, $D=0$. Tracer that crosses the boundary cannot come back.
  • Open end: the same dispersion continues past the boundary. Tracer can cross it back and forth.
  • Name: inlet first, outlet second. Closed–open = closed inlet, open outlet.

Split the number

Why the gas curve falls, then flattens, and liquids sit higher

Lecture 12 · Part A · dispersion

Key Takeaways — dispersion model

1

Describe small deviations from plug flow with one number, $D/uL$: 0 is plug, ∞ is mixed.

2

Below 0.01, read $D/uL=\sigma_\theta^2/2$ off a pulse or step response.

3

Above 0.01, name the vessel’s ends before matching $\sigma_\theta^2$, and check the curve’s shape first.

4

Estimate $D/uL=(D/ud)(d/L)$ from the charts before anything is built.

Lecture 12 · Part B · tanks-in-series

Key Takeaways — tanks-in-series model

1

Model a vessel as $N$ equal stirred tanks; read $N$ from $\sigma_\theta^2=1/N$.

2

Near plug flow the two models agree, $N\approx uL/2D$; choose by kinetics and by what is measured.
